\begin{abstract}
Diffusion-based monocular depth estimators are accurate on smooth surfaces but blur
the depth discontinuities at object boundaries. Sharpening the predicted depth at
the contours found by a segmentation model is a natural remedy, yet applying it at
every contour lowers boundary F1 by 1.9 percent. Observing that this loss arises
from sharpening contours that carry no depth step, we propose to keep the
sharpening operation fixed and to learn only which contours it should be applied
to. On NYUv2 the method improves boundary F1 by 11.9 percent averaged over four
seeds, and the same selector, applied without retraining to a depth model of a
different architecture and output representation, improves it by 27.8 percent. We
also report that the selector does not transfer across datasets.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
monocular depth estimation, depth boundaries, post-processing, diffusion models
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}

Recent work repurposes the prior knowledge of large image generation models for
monocular depth estimation. Marigold~\cite{marigold} fine-tunes the image diffusion
model Stable Diffusion on synthetic RGB-D data and obtains a depth estimator that
generalises well from a small amount of training data. Lotus~\cite{lotus} develops
this further, replacing the multi-step diffusion process with a single-step
prediction and accelerating inference substantially while keeping its accuracy.
Marigold V2~\cite{marigoldv2} raises accuracy further by building on a diffusion
transformer for image editing.

These methods, however, blur the depth discontinuity at object boundaries. This is
thought to stem from the smoothness prior of the generative model and from
predicting in a latent space of reduced resolution, and it spreads the step at an
occlusion boundary over several pixels. Pixel-averaged metrics such as AbsRel
hardly register the degradation, since boundary pixels are a small fraction of the
image; it becomes apparent under boundary-specific metrics~\cite{depthpro}.

Improving boundaries by post-processing is attractive because it leaves the depth
model untouched and applies to the output of any model. Prior work has learned how
to correct the depth, through a displacement field that re-samples pixels around
occlusion boundaries~\cite{ramamonjisoa} or through boundary-preserving disparity
synthesis~\cite{edgeguided}.

A simpler remedy draws on image segmentation. A segmentation model such as Segment
Anything~\cite{sam} partitions an image into objects and regions and extracts their
contours with high accuracy, and the contour of an object often coincides with a
depth discontinuity. The predicted depth can then be refilled from both sides of a
contour, turning a blurred step into a sharp one; we call this sharpening. Applied
at every contour on Marigold V2, however, it lowers boundary \bfone{} by 1.9\%.
Most segmentation contours (79.8\%), such as wallpaper patterns and floor seams,
are contours that carry no depth step, and sharpening there introduces a
discontinuity the scene does not have.

This suggests that whether sharpening helps is decided less by where the contours
lie than by which of them it is applied to. Indeed, selecting with ground truth
only the contours that lie on true depth steps turns the effect into a gain without
moving any contour. We therefore keep the sharpening operation fixed and learn only
which contours to apply it to. The selector is a small U-Net that takes the RGB
image, the predicted depth and the contours as input, while the depth model and the
segmentation model stay frozen. Since the selector is the only trained component,
the improvement is attributable to learning the selection. On NYUv2 the method
improves boundary \bfone{} by 11.9\%, and the same selector, applied to Lotus
without retraining, improves it by 27.8\%.

Our contributions are threefold:

\begin{enumerate}
\item A post-processing method that fixes the sharpening operation and learns only
      which contours to apply it to (Sec.~\ref{sec:method}).
\item Evidence that it improves boundary accuracy beyond training-free controls and
      classical edge-preserving filters (Sec.~\ref{sec:experiments}).
\item Evidence that it transfers across depth models without retraining but not
      across datasets (Sec.~\ref{sec:experiments}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Diffusion-based monocular depth.} Beyond the models above, one-step
fine-tuning was shown to simplify these models and accelerate them by orders of
magnitude~\cite{onestep}. We evaluate on two that are as far apart as the family
allows: Marigold V2, a 21.3B-parameter diffusion transformer fine-tuned with 4-bit
QLoRA that emits affine-invariant log depth, and Lotus, an 868M-parameter UNet that
emits disparity.

\textbf{Boundary sharpening as post-processing.} Ramamonjisoa
et al.~\cite{ramamonjisoa} is the closest prior work: it applies to any depth
estimator's output and uses NYUv2 with the same 795 training and 654 test frames.
SharpNet~\cite{sharpnet} instead builds occluding-contour recovery into the depth
network's training, and depth refinement through neural radiance
fields~\cite{nerfrefine} fuses a monocular estimate with a reconstructed field. To
our knowledge none fixes the repair and learns only its target, and none uses a
promptable segmentation model as the source of candidate boundaries;
Segment Anything~\cite{sam} has been used the other way round, to refine masks
with depth as a prior.

\textbf{We do not compare directly against~\cite{ramamonjisoa}.} No trained
weights are released and the public implementation does not run under a current
environment, so a direct comparison is out of reach here. Reimplementing it on
the same split is left as future work.

\textbf{Boundary metrics.}
We adopt the scale-invariant boundary \bfone{} of Depth Pro~\cite{depthpro},
which declares a depth step between neighbouring pixels when their depth
\emph{ratio} exceeds a threshold, and sweeps that threshold over eleven values
from 5\% to 25\%. We propose no new metric. Neither Lotus nor Marigold V2
reports \bfone{} on NYUv2, so we measure the baselines ourselves.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centerline{\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/pipeline.png}}

model are frozen, and the repair is a fixed operation with no parameters, so
the gain is attributable to the selection alone.}
\label{fig:pipeline}
\end{figure}

Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline} shows the pipeline. The depth model and the
segmentation model are frozen. The repair is the fixed operation of
Sec.~\ref{sec:repair}, which learns nothing. The only trained component is the
selector; Sec.~\ref{sec:why} motivates this design.

\subsection{The repair operation}
\label{sec:repair}

We call the operation \emph{barrier sharpening}: it treats each selected contour
as a barrier across which depth may not be interpolated. Let $z$ be a predicted
depth map, $V$ its valid pixels and $C \subseteq V$ a set of contour pixels. The
operation discards the band
\begin{equation}
B = \{\, p \in V \mid \mathrm{dist}(p, C) \le r_b \,\}, \qquad r_b = 2,
\end{equation}
labels the connected components of $V \setminus B$, so that the two sides of a
contour receive different labels, and floods those labels inward one pixel at a
time until $B$ is covered, giving every $p \in B$ a label $\ell(p)$. Each
discarded pixel is then refilled from its own side only,
\begin{equation}
\hat z(p) = \frac{1}{|N(p)|} \sum_{q \in N(p)} z(q),
\end{equation}
where $N(p) = \{\, q \notin B \mid \|q - p\| \le r_f,\ \ell(q) = \ell(p) \,\}$
and $r_f = 3$. Pixels outside $B$ are unchanged. The band is discarded because a diffusion model
interpolates across the boundary; refilling from one side only removes that
mixing. The component labels come from the contours themselves, not from the
segmentation masks. The operation has two settings and learns nothing.
We require $r_f > r_b$, or $N(p)$ is empty and the
operation leaves the map unchanged.

The separation of the two sides holds only where a contour closes or reaches the
image border. Where it ends inside the image the valid region stays connected
around that end, so both sides of the band belong to one component there and
one-sided interpolation does not hold. Selection makes this more common:
keeping 6\% of the contour pixels leaves fragments rather than whole outlines.
Both effects weaken the repair, and both are present in every number we report.

\subsection{Applying it everywhere fails}
\label{sec:why}

Applied at every contour that Segment Anything draws on NYUv2, the operation
lowers Marigold V2's boundary \bfone{} by 1.90\% (Table~\ref{tab:diagnosis}).
This is specific to a model whose boundaries are already reasonable: on Lotus,
whose boundaries start blunter (baseline \bfone{} 0.0693 against 0.0884), the
same unselected sharpening \emph{gains} 10.31\% (Table~\ref{tab:controls}).

\subsection{Localisation is not the problem}

Measuring from each true depth discontinuity to the nearest contour gives a
median of 1.00\,px, with 66.9\% within 1\,px and 76.9\% within 2\,px. The
contours are where the boundaries are, but 79.8\% of the contour pixels have no depth step anywhere within an eight-pixel
window; these are wallpaper patterns, floor seams and poster edges. Sharpening there
introduces a discontinuity into a region that has none.

\subsection{Selecting with ground truth reverses the sign}
\label{sec:gtsel}

The 79.8\% uses a generous eight-pixel window; the selection below uses a strict
one-pixel tolerance. Of the 22{,}167 segmentation
contour pixels in an average frame (10.6\% of its valid pixels) 1{,}345,
or 6.07\%, lie within 1\,px of a true discontinuity. An oracle that keeps exactly
those, and leaves every kept contour where it is, moves the effect from
$-1.90\%$ to $+43.39\%$
(Table~\ref{tab:diagnosis}). The contours are not moved in this experiment; only the set they are drawn from
differs. The loss is therefore attributable to which contours are sharpened rather
than to where they lie, and Sec.~\ref{sec:selector} learns that selection.

\subsection{The selector}
\label{sec:selector}

\textbf{Inputs and output.} The selector is a four-level U-Net with 1.9M
parameters. It takes the RGB image (3 channels), the predicted depth (1), and
the contour mask (1), and emits one logit per pixel. The prediction is
standardised per image. Without that step no error is raised when the level and
scale differ between training and inference; only the final metric degrades, and
it does so quietly. For a model that emits disparity $v$ rather than log depth
we apply $-\log\max(v, 10^{-3})$ first: disparity rises as depth falls, so
feeding it raw would invert the signal, and an affine-invariant disparity can be
non-positive, which the clamp handles. This is monotone in depth but not an
affine map of log depth: the unknown shift in $v$ survives the logarithm as a
nonlinearity that per-image standardisation cannot remove. We apply the
selector unchanged regardless, and the Lotus column of Table~\ref{tab:main} is
what that approximation is worth.

\textbf{Retention.} We keep the top 6\% of contour pixels per image rather than
thresholding the probability. A fixed threshold keeps a different amount
depending on how many contours a scene happens to contain, so the budget the
repair spends would drift from frame to frame. The budget is 6\% because that is
the positive rate on the training frames, where 6.066\% of contour pixels lie
within 1\,px of a true step.

\textbf{Loss and training.} The selector is trained with binary cross-entropy
averaged over the contour pixels only, so the 89\% of the valid image that carries
no contour is not a question the selector is asked. A contour pixel is positive if it lies within 1\,px of a depth
discontinuity in ground truth, where a discontinuity is a depth ratio above
10\%. We chose that threshold on a validation split rather than on the test
set: scoring \bfone{} on the 141 frames of the 40 scenes held out of the 795
training frames, 10\% gives $+12.74\%$ against $+8.66$, $+6.29$ and $+5.28\%$
for 15, 20 and 25\%. Raising it thins the positives (from 6.07\% of contour
pixels to 2.13\%) so the fixed budget is then spent mostly on negatives.
Every hyperparameter of the method, the threshold and the retention alike, was set
on the validation split; the one exception is a control, and Sec.~\ref{sec:controls}
states it there. Ground truth is used only to generate these labels and never
appears in the inference path. The 795
NYUv2 training frames are split by scene into 654 for training and 141 for
validation; the deployed checkpoint is the best-validation epoch and has seen
only those 654, which are a different set from the 654 test frames. Training
takes 16 minutes and 3.5\,GB of VRAM on one RTX 4090 and never touches the test
set.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Setup}

We evaluate on the 654-frame NYUv2~\cite{nyuv2} test split, scene-disjoint from
the training frames. The two transfer sets of Sec.~\ref{sec:transfer} are the
800-frame ScanNet~\cite{scannet} validation sample used by~\cite{marigoldv2} and
a 300-frame scene-level hold-out of Hypersim~\cite{hypersim}. All three store
depth as 16-bit millimetres and are read with each dataset's own validity mask
and depth range; NYUv2 additionally uses the Eigen crop. The metric is the
scale-invariant boundary \bfone{} of Depth Pro~\cite{depthpro}. We report the
relative change in \bfone{} against each base model's own unsharpened
prediction. Predictions are aligned to ground truth in their own space before
scoring (log depth for Marigold V2, disparity for Lotus) and the alignment
affects every variant equally.

\textbf{Choice of benchmarks.} \bfone{} compares neighbouring
pixels, so it needs ground truth dense enough for both to be valid, and three of
the five benchmarks these models are evaluated on do not provide it: KITTI
\cite{kitti} leaves 13.2\% of adjacent pairs valid and ETH3D \cite{eth3d} 4.1\%,
while DIODE \cite{diode} is dense but its evaluation protocol masks the depth
gradients this method operates on, removing 99.4\% of indoor discontinuities. We
therefore evaluate on NYUv2 and ScanNet, and additionally on Hypersim as a third
transfer set.

\textbf{Implementation.} The selector is trained with AdamW at a learning rate of
$3 \times 10^{-4}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$ and a cosine schedule, for 30 epochs at
batch size 4. Frames are used at their native $480 \times 640$ with no resizing and
no augmentation: the band and the refill radius are defined in pixels, so rescaling
would change what $r_b = 2$ means. Contours come from Segment Anything
\cite{sam} with the ViT-H checkpoint in automatic mode, a $48 \times 48$ prompt
grid and its default quality filter; ``grid 16'' and ``grid 32'' in
Sec.~\ref{sec:cost} vary that grid. A frame's contour set is the union of the
one-pixel boundaries of the returned masks, not the union of the masks, which
would fill the frame. Timings are on one RTX 4090.

disparity-to-log-depth conversion; the deviation reported there is the spread of
those four selectors on Lotus, not of four Lotus-trained models.

\subsection{A second boundary metric}

An improvement visible in one metric may be an improvement in that metric's
definition. We therefore also report Soft Edge Error \cite{smdnets}, the
boundary metric Marigold V2 adopts, transcribed from its implementation.
SEE$_k$ averages, over ground-truth boundary pixels, the smallest absolute error
between the prediction and any ground-truth depth in a $k\times k$
neighbourhood; it forgives a displaced boundary and punishes a blurred one.
Unlike \bfone{} it is an absolute error in depth units, so it is not
scale-invariant and the prediction must be aligned first. Lower is better.

The two metrics agree on every variant of both models: the selector improves
both, the relocated control worsens both, and sharpening at GT contours is best

Their magnitudes are not comparable and should not be read as such. SEE is an
absolute error averaged over boundary pixels, floored by how accurate the
model's depth values are there, so sharpening at GT contours raises \bfone{} by

$k$ grows (from $-11.44\%$ to $-2.91\%$ at GT contours) which is what a
metric that forgives more displacement at larger $k$ should do.

\label{sec:controls}

simply being sparse. Table~\ref{tab:controls} measures six training-free
alternatives, each on the base model's own predictions.

The edge-preserving filter row is the classical alternative: a guided or joint
bilateral filter with the image as guide. Both lose on both models, monotonically
in the radius, from $-4.75\%$ and $-7.15\%$ at the gentlest setting to
$-50.14\%$ and $-45.30\%$. A filter can only smooth,
and a smoothed boundary is what \bfone{} counts against; discarding the band and
refilling each side from itself is what restores a step instead.

The hand-crafted control thresholds a local descriptor of the predicted depth's
log-gradient. We select its feature by the final metric on the test split rather
than by ranking quality on held-out data: over four candidates the AUC ordering
and the \bfone{} ordering are \emph{inverted}, and the feature with the worst
AUC (0.801) gives the best \bfone{} ($+1.25\%$) while the best AUC (0.876) gives
the worst ($-2.59\%$). That gives the control an advantage the method does not
have, which makes the comparison conservative; a control chosen by a proxy
metric would have been too weak, and a weak control only flatters the method it
is compared against.

Both random rows spend exactly the budget the selector spends, taken from its
output frame by frame. Random-pixel selection is close on the two models
($-24.96\%$ and $-22.11\%$), which is what a control that measures the repair
operation rather than the depth model should do. Random blocks, which keeps the
contour contiguous, costs almost nothing on either and nothing at all on Lotus:
scattering the selection is most of what makes the pixel control so destructive,
and choosing badly on its own incurs almost no cost.

\subsection{How much the segmentation contributes}

Removing the segmentation model entirely, so that the selector must find
boundaries itself from RGB and depth and every valid pixel is a candidate, gives
$+7.30\%$. Both halves of this ablation come from a single seed, so the figure
to compare against is that seed's $+12.49\%$: the segmentation is worth 5.19
points, 42\% of that run's gain. This contribution arises on the recall side rather than the precision side. On
segmentation contours the true steps number 1{,}345 pixels per frame and the
budget of 1{,}330 can cover 98.9\% of them; counting over the whole valid image
there are 2{,}941, and the same budget caps recall at 45.2\%. The segmentation
earns its contribution by \emph{shrinking the target set}.

\subsection{Cross-dataset generalisation}
\label{sec:transfer}

The selector transfers across depth models but not across datasets.
Table~\ref{tab:generalisation} applies the NYUv2-trained selector to ScanNet and
Hypersim, using Marigold V2 throughout.

On neither held-out dataset does the selector beat using every contour. What
fails is the transfer, not the datasets. A selector trained on 10{,}000 Hypersim
frames reaches $+5.05\%$ on Hypersim itself, against $+2.28\%$ for no selection,
at 88.1\% precision on a 22.8\% base rate; the same checkpoint reaches
$-2.23\%$ on NYUv2 and $+0.39\%$ on ScanNet. Hypersim is not saturated either (sharpening at its own GT contours gives $+39.49\%$) so the method works in
its domain as it does in NYUv2's, and stops working only when the domain changes
between training and use.

Three further remedies failed: dropping the RGB channels, sweeping the retention
from 2\% to 20\% on ScanNet, and training on an equal mixture of 795 NYUv2 and
795 Hypersim frames. Data volume does not account for the gap either. Cutting
NYUv2 to 393 frames costs 2.2 points of gain ($+11.92\%$ to $+9.72\%$, two
seeds), against the 9.1 points that separate in-domain from ScanNet.

\textbf{Precision does not order the rows.} The NYUv2-trained selector is more
precise on Hypersim than in its own domain (55.8\% against 50.2\%) yet scores
worst there, and precision is not comparable across datasets in any case: the
share of contour pixels on a real step is 6.07\% on NYUv2 and 22.8\% on
Hypersim. What separates the rows appears to be whether the evaluation data
matches the training domain, which we cannot reduce to a measured quantity.

Post-processing that is model-agnostic is not thereby dataset-agnostic.

after warm-up on real frames. Marigold V2's transformer is loaded in 4-bit NF4,
the released configuration's default, so its 21.3 billion parameters occupy
roughly 10.6\,GB and the model fits a 24\,GB card; at bf16 it would not.

The selector costs 12.0\,ms and the repair 18.3\,ms, so what this work adds is
30.3\,ms, 6\% of the depth model's own 495\,ms. The segmentation dominates
everything else, at 534\,ms to 2{,}603\,ms depending on its point grid.

\textbf{A coarse grid gives a comparable gain.} Grid 16 reaches 92\% of grid 48's gain for
20\% of the segmentation cost, making the whole pipeline 3.0$\times$ faster and
cutting the time per point of \bfone{} improvement to about one in 2.7. The 0.90-point gap equals
the standard deviation across seeds, so a single pair of runs cannot settle the
ordering; paired by seed, however, grid 48 is ahead in all four, by
$0.90 \pm 0.29$ points (paired $t$-test, $p = 0.009$). The difference is consistent and small. Precision runs the other way across
the grids (51.6\%, 50.6\%, 50.2\% at grids 16, 32, 48, one seed), which says
what the coarse grid is doing: it traces large objects and misses wallpaper patterns and
floor seams, so a larger share of the candidates it produces are true boundaries. Grid 48 finds
3{,}200 more contour pixels per frame than grid 16 and yields 0.90 points of
\bfone{} with them, so most of those are false positives. This is the same mechanism as the
ablation above (the segmentation helps by shrinking the target set)
appearing along a different axis.

\section{Conclusion}

On a depth model whose boundaries are already reasonable, post-hoc sharpening
fails because most segmentation contours mark no depth step. Fixing the repair
and learning only its target makes the gain attributable and transfers across
depth models without retraining, but not across datasets.

\textbf{Limitations.} The oracle selection of Sec.~\ref{sec:gtsel} reaches
$+43.39\%$ against our $+11.92\%$; the gap is precision, 50.2\% against 100\%.
AbsRel and $\delta_1$ move too little to claim, and we claim only that they do
not regress. Supervision and \bfone{} both refer to ground-truth depth steps;
ground truth never enters inference, but the shared definition may bear on how
the result is read. Making the selection independent of the training domain is the main open problem.
