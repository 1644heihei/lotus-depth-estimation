\begin{abstract}
Diffusion-based monocular depth estimators produce accurate surfaces but blurred
depth discontinuities. A natural remedy is to sharpen the predicted depth at
contours found by a segmentation model, yet applying a barrier-sharpening
operation at every contour lowers boundary F1 by 1.90 percent on NYUv2. We show
by measurement that the bottleneck is not where the contours are, since a true
depth step is a median of one pixel from the nearest contour, but which contours
to use: 79.8 percent of them have no depth step anywhere within an eight-pixel
window. Keeping with ground truth only the 6.07 percent that lie within one pixel
of a true step, without moving a single contour, turns a loss of 1.90 percent
into a gain of 43.39 percent. We therefore fix the repair operation and learn
only where to apply it. A selector of 1.9 million parameters, trained for 16
minutes on 654 NYUv2 frames, raises boundary F1 by 11.92 percent over four
seeds with a standard deviation of 0.90, and the same four selectors, applied
with no retraining to a second depth model of a different architecture and
output representation, raise its boundary F1 by 27.83 percent with a standard
deviation of 1.27. Five training-free controls, measured on each model's own
predictions, place the learning's contribution at 10.7 and 17.4 points. The
selector and the repair add 30 milliseconds per frame on top of the depth
model's 495, and the segmentation they depend on at least 534 more. We also report a
negative result: the selector transfers across depth models but not across
datasets, and the remedies we tried failed to fix it.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Monocular depth estimators built on pre-trained diffusion
models~\cite{marigold,lotus,marigoldv2} reach strong pixel-wise accuracy, but
they inherit the smoothness of the generative prior at object boundaries. A
depth map can have excellent AbsRel while every occlusion boundary in it is
spread over several pixels. Pixel-averaged metrics do not see this: the
boundary pixels are a small fraction of the image, so a metric dominated by
flat surfaces reports no problem. Boundary-specific metrics do~\cite{depthpro}.

Fixing boundaries after the fact is attractive because it leaves the depth
model untouched. Prior work learns \emph{how} to move the
depth~\cite{ramamonjisoa,sharpnet,edgeguided}. We take the opposite
decomposition: fix the repair, and learn \emph{where} it should act.

The reason is empirical. Section~\ref{sec:diagnosis} measures a
barrier-sharpening operation applied at every contour a segmentation
model~\cite{sam} draws, and finds it \emph{costs} 1.90\% of boundary \bfone{}
on Marigold V2~\cite{marigoldv2}. The same measurement shows why: contour
localisation is already good enough, while 79.8\% of contours mark no depth
step at all, and sharpening there only flattens smooth surface. Selecting the correct
contours with ground truth, with no change to their positions, moves the effect
from $-1.90\%$ to $+43.39\%$. Selection, not localisation, is the binding
constraint.

Our contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
\item A measurement that identifies selection as the bottleneck in post-hoc
      boundary sharpening, including an oracle that reverses the sign of the
      effect without moving any contour (Sec.~\ref{sec:diagnosis}).
\item A method that fixes the repair operation and learns only the selection, so
      that what the learning contributes is directly attributable
      (Sec.~\ref{sec:method}).
\item Results on two depth models that differ in architecture, parameter count
      and output representation, with four seeds and five training-free
      controls measured on each model's own predictions
      (Sec.~\ref{sec:experiments}).
\item A cost analysis showing the added work is 30\,ms per frame and that a
      coarse segmentation grid gives 92\% of the gain at 20\% of the
      segmentation cost (Sec.~\ref{sec:cost}).
\end{enumerate}

We also report, in Sec.~\ref{sec:limitations}, that the selector does not
transfer across datasets, and that our attempts to fix that failed.

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Diffusion-based monocular depth.}
Marigold~\cite{marigold} repurposes Stable Diffusion for affine-invariant
monocular depth by fine-tuning on synthetic RGB-D. Marigold
V2~\cite{marigoldv2} keeps that formulation and replaces the U-Net with a
21.3-billion-parameter diffusion transformer adapted from an image-editing
model, fine-tuned with 4-bit QLoRA and run in a single step.
Lotus~\cite{lotus} removes
the diffusion schedule, predicting in a single step with the timestep pinned.
Subsequent work showed that one-step fine-tuning both simplifies and
accelerates these models by orders of magnitude~\cite{onestep}. We evaluate on
two of them that are as far apart as the family allows: Marigold
V2~\cite{marigoldv2}, a diffusion transformer of 21{,}282{,}180{,}672
parameters emitting affine-invariant log depth, and Lotus~\cite{lotus}, an
868M-parameter UNet emitting disparity.

\textbf{Boundary sharpening as post-processing.}
Ramamonjisoa et al.~\cite{ramamonjisoa} is the closest prior work: it learns a
2D displacement field that re-samples pixels around occlusion boundaries, is
applicable to any depth estimator's output, and uses NYUv2 with the same 795
training and 654 test frames we use. SharpNet~\cite{sharpnet} instead builds
occluding-contour recovery into the depth network's own training.
Edge-guided post-processing~\cite{edgeguided} preserves boundaries during
disparity synthesis in self-supervised pipelines. More recently, depth
refinement through neural radiance fields~\cite{nerfrefine} fuses a monocular
estimate with a reconstructed field to recover geometric detail. All of these
learn how to move depth. None, to our knowledge, fixes the repair and learns
only its target, and none uses a promptable segmentation model as the source of
candidate boundaries.

\textbf{We do not compare directly against~\cite{ramamonjisoa}.} Its code is
public but no trained weights are released, the README documents no inference
procedure, and it is written against the PyTorch 0.4 API, which current PyTorch
no longer provides. We instead ablate our own use of segmentation
(Sec.~\ref{sec:experiments}), which answers the related question of how much
the external segmentation contributes. That is an ablation of our method, not a
comparison against prior work, and we do not present it as one. Since training
here costs 16 minutes, a re-implementation evaluated on this split would be the
right comparison, and it remains to be done.

\textbf{Boundary metrics.}
We adopt the scale-invariant boundary \bfone{} of Depth Pro~\cite{depthpro},
which declares a depth step between neighbouring pixels when their depth
\emph{ratio} exceeds a threshold, and sweeps that threshold over eleven values
from 5\% to 25\%. We propose no new metric. Neither Lotus nor Marigold V2
reports \bfone{} on NYUv2, so we measure the baselines ourselves.

\textbf{Segmentation for depth.} Segment Anything~\cite{sam} has been used to
refine masks with depth as a prior; we use it in the opposite direction, as a
source of candidate depth boundaries that a learned selector then filters.

\section{Diagnosis: selection is the bottleneck}
\label{sec:diagnosis}

\subsection{The repair operation}

We call the operation \emph{barrier sharpening}: it treats each selected
contour as a barrier across which depth may not be interpolated. Let $z$ be a
predicted depth map and $C$ a set of contour pixels. The operation discards a
band of $\pm 2$\,px around $C$; labels the connected components of the valid
region with that band removed, so the two sides of a contour become different
components; floods those labels inward one pixel at a time until the band is
filled, so the two sides meet inside the band rather than one leaking across;
and refills each discarded pixel with the mean of its \emph{own} component's
depth within a radius of 3\,px. The band is discarded because a diffusion model
interpolates across the boundary; refilling from one side only removes that
mixing. The component labels come from the contours themselves, not from the
segmentation masks. The operation has two settings and learns nothing. The
refill radius must exceed the band, or there is nothing to refill from and the
operation is a silent no-op.

The separation of the two sides holds only where a contour closes or reaches the
image border. Where it ends inside the image the valid region stays connected
around that end, so both sides of the band belong to one component there and the
refill is one-sided in name only. Selection makes this more common, not less:
keeping 6\% of the contour pixels leaves fragments rather than whole outlines.
Both effects weaken the repair, and both are present in every number we report.

\subsection{Applying it everywhere fails}

Table~\ref{tab:diagnosis} applies the operation at every contour that Segment
Anything draws on NYUv2, using Marigold V2 as the base. Boundary \bfone{} falls
by 1.90\%. This diagnosis is specific to a model whose boundaries are already
reasonable: on Lotus the same unselected sharpening \emph{gains} 10.31\%
(Table~\ref{tab:controls}), because its boundaries start blunter --- its
baseline \bfone{} is 0.0693 against Marigold V2's 0.0884 --- so there is more to
gain and less to spoil. Selection is what makes the operation safe on the first
model, and it adds substantially on both.

\subsection{Localisation is not the problem}

Measuring from each true depth discontinuity to the nearest contour gives a
median of 1.00\,px, with 66.9\% within 1\,px and 76.9\% within 2\,px. The
contours are where the boundaries are. The failure is in the other direction:
79.8\% of the contour pixels have no depth step anywhere within an eight-pixel
window --- wallpaper patterns, floor seams, poster edges. Sharpening there
discards a band of smooth surface and refills it from two sides that agree,
which can only add noise.

\subsection{Selecting with ground truth reverses the sign}

Two tolerances appear above and they are not complements of one another, so we
state both. The 79.8\% uses an eight-pixel window, which is generous; the
selection below uses one pixel, which is strict. Of the 22{,}167 segmentation
contour pixels in an average frame --- 10.6\% of its valid pixels --- 1{,}345,
or 6.07\%, lie within 1\,px of a true discontinuity. An oracle that keeps exactly
those, and leaves every kept contour where it is, moves the effect from
$-1.90\%$ to $+43.39\%$
(Table~\ref{tab:diagnosis}). No contour moves; only the set changes. This
isolates selection as the binding constraint and sets the target for a learned
selector.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centerline{\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/pipeline.png}}
\caption{Only the selector is trained. The depth model and the segmentation
model are frozen, and the repair is a fixed operation with no parameters, so
the gain is attributable to the selection alone.}
\label{fig:pipeline}
\end{figure}

Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline} shows the pipeline. The depth model and the
segmentation model are frozen. The repair is the fixed operation of
Sec.~\ref{sec:diagnosis}. The only trained component is the selector.

\textbf{Inputs and output.} The selector is a four-level U-Net with 1{,}928{,}993
parameters. It takes the RGB image (3 channels), the predicted depth (1), and
the contour mask (1), and emits one logit per pixel. The prediction is
standardised per image. Without that step no error is raised when the level and
scale differ between training and inference; only the final metric degrades, and
it does so quietly. For a model that emits disparity $v$ rather than log depth
we apply $-\log\max(v, 10^{-3})$ first: disparity rises as depth falls, so
feeding it raw would invert the signal, and an affine-invariant disparity can be
non-positive, which the clamp handles. This is monotone in depth but not an
affine map of log depth: the unknown shift in $v$ survives the logarithm as a
nonlinearity that per-image standardisation cannot remove. We apply the
selector unchanged regardless, and the Lotus column of Table~\ref{tab:main} is
what that approximation is worth.

\textbf{Retention.} We keep the top 6\% of contour pixels per image rather than
thresholding the probability. A fixed threshold keeps a different amount
depending on how many contours a scene happens to contain, so the budget the
repair spends would drift from frame to frame. The budget is 6\% because that is
the positive rate: on the training frames, 6.07\% of contour pixels lie within
1\,px of a true step. It is not fitted to the test split.

\textbf{Loss and training.} Binary cross-entropy masked to contour pixels; the
89\% of the valid image that carries no contour is not a question the selector
is asked. A contour pixel is positive if it lies within 1\,px of a depth
discontinuity in ground truth, where a discontinuity is a depth ratio above
10\%. We chose that threshold on a validation split rather than on the test
set: scoring \bfone{} on the 141 frames of the 40 scenes held out of the 795
training frames, 10\% gives $+12.74\%$ against $+8.66$, $+6.29$ and $+5.28\%$
for 15, 20 and 25\%. Raising it thins the positives --- from 6.07\% of contour
pixels to 2.13\% --- so the fixed budget is then spent mostly on negatives.
Nothing in this work is selected on the test split except where
Sec.~\ref{sec:controls} says otherwise, which is a control and not the method. Ground truth is used
only to generate these labels and never appears in the inference path. The 795
NYUv2 training frames are split by scene into 654 for training and 141 for
validation; the deployed checkpoint is the best-validation epoch and has seen
only those 654, which are a different set from the 654 test frames. Training
takes 16 minutes and 3.5\,GB of VRAM on one RTX 4090 and never touches the test
set.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Setup}

We evaluate on the 654-frame NYUv2~\cite{nyuv2} test split, scene-disjoint from
the training frames. The two transfer sets of Sec.~\ref{sec:limitations} are the
800-frame ScanNet~\cite{scannet} validation sample used by~\cite{marigoldv2} and
a 300-frame scene-level hold-out of Hypersim~\cite{hypersim}. All three store
depth as 16-bit millimetres and are read with each dataset's own validity mask
and depth range; NYUv2 additionally uses the Eigen crop. The metric is the
scale-invariant boundary \bfone{} of Depth Pro~\cite{depthpro}. We report the
relative change in \bfone{} against each base model's own unsharpened
prediction. Predictions are aligned to ground truth in their own space before
scoring --- log depth for Marigold V2, disparity for Lotus --- and the alignment
affects every variant equally.

\subsection{Main result}

Table~\ref{tab:main} gives the result on two depth models that share no
architecture, no parameter scale and no output representation. Four selectors
are trained, differing only in seed, all on Marigold V2's predictions. The same
four are applied to Lotus with no retraining, adding only the
disparity-to-log-depth conversion; the deviation reported there is the spread of
those four selectors on Lotus, not of four Lotus-trained models.

All four seeds clear $+10\%$ on Marigold V2, with a 95\% confidence interval

slightly worse. The three frames excluded here have a baseline
\bfone{} of zero at all eleven thresholds, so a relative gain is undefined for
them; they are included in the pooled figure, where they contribute zero.

\subsection{A second boundary metric}

An improvement visible in one metric may be an improvement in that metric's
definition. We therefore also report Soft Edge Error, the boundary metric
Marigold V2's own authors use, transcribed from their implementation.
SEE$_k$ averages, over ground-truth boundary pixels, the smallest absolute error
between the prediction and any ground-truth depth in a $k\times k$
neighbourhood; it forgives a displaced boundary and punishes a blurred one.
Unlike \bfone{} it is an absolute error in depth units, so it is not
scale-invariant and the prediction must be aligned first. Lower is better.

metric that forgives more displacement at larger $k$ should do.

\label{sec:controls}

The hand-crafted control thresholds a local descriptor of the predicted depth's
log-gradient. We select its feature by the final metric on the test split rather
than by ranking quality on held-out data: over four candidates the AUC ordering
and the \bfone{} ordering are \emph{inverted}, and the feature with the worst
AUC (0.801) gives the best \bfone{} ($+1.25\%$) while the best AUC (0.876) gives
the worst ($-2.59\%$). That gives the control an advantage the method does not
have, which makes the comparison conservative; a control chosen by a proxy
metric would have been too weak, and a weak control only flatters the method it
is compared against.

Both random rows spend exactly the budget the selector spends, taken from its
output frame by frame. Random-pixel selection is close on the two models
($-24.96\%$ and $-22.11\%$), which is what a control that measures the repair
operation rather than the depth model should do. Random blocks, which keeps the
contour contiguous, costs almost nothing on either and nothing at all on Lotus:
scattering the selection is most of what makes the pixel control so destructive,
and choosing badly on its own is nearly free.

\subsection{How much the segmentation contributes}

Removing the segmentation model entirely, so that the selector must find
boundaries itself from RGB and depth and every valid pixel is a candidate, gives
$+7.30\%$. Both halves of this ablation come from a single seed, so the figure
to compare against is that seed's $+12.49\%$: the segmentation is worth 5.19
points, 42\% of that run's gain. The mechanism is recall, not precision. On
segmentation contours the true steps number 1{,}345 pixels per frame and the
budget of 1{,}330 can cover 98.9\% of them; counting over the whole valid image
there are 2{,}941, and the same budget caps recall at 45.2\%. The segmentation
earns its contribution by \emph{shrinking the target set}.

The selector costs 12.0\,ms and the repair 18.3\,ms, so what this work adds is

\textbf{A coarse grid suffices.} Grid 16 reaches 92\% of grid 48's gain for
20\% of the segmentation cost, making the whole pipeline 3.0$\times$ faster and
2.7$\times$ cheaper per point of \bfone{}. The 0.90-point gap is smaller than
the 0.90 standard deviation across seeds, so no single pair of runs could settle
the ordering; paired by seed, however, grid 48 is ahead in all four, by
$0.90 \pm 0.29$ points ($p = 0.009$). The difference is consistent and small. Precision runs the other way across
the grids (51.6\%, 50.6\%, 50.2\% at grids 16, 32, 48, one seed), which says
what the coarse grid is doing: it traces large objects and misses wallpaper patterns and
floor seams, so the candidate set it produces is already cleaner. Grid 48 finds
3{,}200 more contour pixels per frame than grid 16 and buys 0.90 points of
\bfone{} with them, so most of those are false positives. This is the same mechanism as the
ablation above --- the segmentation helps by shrinking the target set ---
appearing along a different axis.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\textbf{Two public benchmarks, because a boundary metric needs dense ground
truth.} The base models are evaluated on five benchmarks: NYUv2, KITTI,
ScanNet, ETH3D and DIODE. \bfone{} compares NEIGHBOURING pixels' depth ratio, so
it needs ground truth dense enough for both to exist, and on three of the five
it is not. KITTI's LiDAR leaves 13.2\% of adjacent pairs with both pixels valid;
ETH3D, which projects a laser point cloud onto 24-megapixel DSLR frames, leaves
4.1\%. DIODE is dense (99.7\% indoors), but its standard evaluation protocol
masks out pixels whose depth gradient exceeds a threshold, because its laser
ground truth is unreliable at edges --- and that filter removes 99.4\% of the
depth discontinuities indoors and 100\% outdoors, which is precisely what this
method operates on. Of the five, \bfone{} is well defined on NYUv2 and ScanNet,
and we evaluate on both. We also evaluate on Hypersim, which is not one of the
five but is Marigold V2's own training domain, as a third transfer test.

\textbf{The selector transfers across depth models but not across datasets.}
Table~\ref{tab:generalisation} applies the NYUv2-trained selector to ScanNet and
Hypersim, using Marigold V2 throughout.

On neither held-out dataset does the selector beat using every contour. What
fails is the transfer, not the datasets. A selector trained on 10{,}000 Hypersim
frames reaches $+5.05\%$ on Hypersim itself, against $+2.28\%$ for no selection,
at 88.1\% precision on a 22.8\% base rate; the same checkpoint reaches
$-2.23\%$ on NYUv2 and $+0.39\%$ on ScanNet. Hypersim is not saturated either
--- sharpening at its own GT contours gives $+39.49\%$ --- so the method works in
its domain as it does in NYUv2's, and stops working only when the domain changes
between training and use.

Three further remedies failed. Dropping the RGB channels made transfer worse
rather than better. Sweeping the retention from 2\% to 20\% on ScanNet found no
setting that reaches the no-selection result. Training on an equal mixture of 795
NYUv2 and 795 Hypersim frames damaged the in-domain result without helping
transfer. Data volume cannot explain the transfer failure, though it is not free:
cutting NYUv2 to 393 frames costs 3.2 points of precision (50.2\% to 47.0\%) and
2.2 points of gain ($+11.92\%$ to $+9.72\%$), 18\% of it, measured over two
seeds against the four of Table~\ref{tab:main}. The gap between in-domain and
ScanNet is 9.1 points, four times larger.

\textbf{Precision does not order the rows.} The NYUv2-trained selector is more
precise on Hypersim than in its own domain (55.8\% against 50.2\%) and scores
worst there. Precision is not comparable across datasets in any case, since the
share of contour pixels sitting on a real step differs between them --- 6.07\% on
NYUv2 against 22.8\% on Hypersim. What separates the rows is whether the
evaluation data matches the selector's training domain, and we cannot reduce
that to a quantity we measure.

We report this because it bears on a claim the prior work in this area makes.
Post-processing that is model-agnostic is not thereby dataset-agnostic, and the
two kinds of generalisation evidently come apart.

\textbf{Headroom.} The oracle selection of Sec.~\ref{sec:diagnosis} reaches
$+43.39\%$ against our $+11.92\%$, so 72.5\% of what selection could deliver is
unrecovered. The gap is precision: 50.2\% against the oracle's 100\%.

\textbf{Metric scope.} The improvement is in boundary metrics. AbsRel and
$\delta_1$ move by amounts too small to claim as improvements, and we claim
only that they do not regress.

\textbf{Label and metric share a definition.} Supervision is ``within 1\,px of a
ground-truth depth step'' and \bfone{} scores agreement with ground-truth depth
steps. Ground truth never enters inference and the test scenes are disjoint
from training, but the correspondence should be read with that in mind.

\section{Conclusion}

Post-hoc boundary sharpening for monocular depth fails, on a model whose
boundaries are already reasonable, not because contours are badly localised but
because most of them mark no depth step. Fixing the repair and learning only its
target makes the gain attributable, costs 30\,ms per frame and 1.9M parameters,
and transfers across depth models without retraining; the segmentation it
depends on costs a further 534\,ms at the coarsest grid. It does not transfer
across datasets, and we were unable to make it do so.
