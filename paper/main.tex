\begin{abstract}
Diffusion-based monocular depth estimators are accurate on smooth surfaces but blur
the depth discontinuities at object boundaries. A natural remedy is to sharpen the
predicted depth at the contours found by a segmentation model, yet applying it at
every contour lowers boundary F1. Observing that this loss arises from sharpening
contours that carry no depth step, we propose to keep the sharpening operation
fixed and to learn only which contours it should be applied to. In experiments,
the method improves boundary F1 on NYUv2 by 11.9 percent, and the same selector,
applied without retraining to a depth model of a different architecture and output
representation, improves it by 27.8 percent.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
monocular depth estimation, depth boundaries, segmentation, diffusion models
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}

Recent work repurposes the prior knowledge of large image generation models for
monocular depth estimation. Marigold~\cite{marigold} fine-tunes the image diffusion
model Stable Diffusion on synthetic RGB-D data and obtains a depth estimator that
generalises well from a small amount of training data. Lotus~\cite{lotus} develops
this further, replacing the multi-step diffusion process with a single-step
prediction and accelerating inference substantially while keeping its accuracy.
Marigold V2~\cite{marigoldv2} raises accuracy further by building on a diffusion
transformer for image editing.

These methods, however, blur the depth discontinuities at object boundaries. This
is thought to stem from the smoothness prior of the generative model and from
predicting in a latent space of reduced resolution, and the step at an occlusion
boundary can spread over several pixels. Metrics computed over the whole image
hardly register this degradation, since boundary pixels are a small fraction of
the image, but boundary-specific metrics reveal it~\cite{depthpro}.

To address blurred depth discontinuities, methods that improve boundaries by
post-processing have been proposed. They leave the depth model untouched and
apply to the output of any model. Prior work has learned how to correct the
depth, through a displacement field that re-samples pixels around occlusion
boundaries~\cite{ramamonjisoa} or through edge-guided
post-processing~\cite{edgeguided}.

A simpler remedy draws on image segmentation. A segmentation model such as Segment
Anything~\cite{sam} partitions an image into objects and regions and extracts their
contours with high accuracy, and the contour of an object often coincides with a
depth discontinuity. The predicted depth can then be refilled from both sides of a
contour, turning a blurred step into a sharp one; we call this sharpening. Applied
at every contour on Marigold V2, however, it lowers boundary \bfone{}. Most
segmentation contours, such as wallpaper patterns and floor seams, carry no depth
step, and sharpening there introduces a discontinuity the scene does not have.

This suggests that whether sharpening helps is decided less by where the contours
lie than by which of them it is applied to. Indeed, selecting with ground truth
only the contours that lie on true depth steps turns the effect into a gain without
moving any contour.

We therefore keep the sharpening operation fixed and learn only which contours to
apply it to. The selector is a small U-Net that takes the RGB image, the predicted
depth and the contours as input, while the depth model and the segmentation model
are left unchanged. Since the selector is the only trained component, the
improvement is attributable to learning the selection.

Our contributions are threefold:

\begin{enumerate}
\item We propose a post-processing method that fixes the sharpening operation and
      learns only which contours to apply it to.
\item We show that it improves boundary accuracy beyond training-free controls and
      classical edge-preserving filters.
\item We show that it transfers across depth models without retraining but not
      across datasets.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Diffusion-based monocular depth estimation}

Marigold~\cite{marigold} fine-tunes the image diffusion model Stable Diffusion on
synthetic RGB-D data to estimate affine-invariant depth. Lotus~\cite{lotus}
predicts the depth itself rather than noise and replaces the multi-step process
with a single step at a fixed timestep, improving both accuracy and speed. Garcia
et al.~\cite{onestep} showed that correcting Marigold's inference procedure and
combining single-step inference with end-to-end fine-tuning makes it orders of
magnitude faster without loss of accuracy. Marigold V2~\cite{marigoldv2} builds
on a diffusion transformer for image editing and estimates affine-invariant log
depth in a single step. All of these improve the depth model itself; this work
leaves the model untouched and post-processes its output.

\subsection{Improving depth boundaries}

Ramamonjisoa et al.~\cite{ramamonjisoa} learn a 2D displacement field that
re-samples pixels around occlusion boundaries, improving the output of any depth
estimator by post-processing. It uses NYUv2 with the same 795 training and 654
test frames and is the closest prior work. SharpNet~\cite{sharpnet} builds
occluding-contour estimation into the training of the depth network, and Peng
et al.~\cite{edgeguided} reduce the degradation near occlusion boundaries in
self-supervised monocular depth with an edge-guided post-processing step.
Muthukkumar~\cite{nerfrefine} fuses a monocular estimate with depth from a neural
radiance field to recover detail. All of these design or learn how to correct the
depth; this work fixes the correction and learns only where to apply it. Since no
trained weights are released for~\cite{ramamonjisoa}, we do not compare against
it directly.

\subsection{Segmentation and depth estimation}

Segment Anything~\cite{sam} is a segmentation model that extracts arbitrary
regions of an image without being told the object class. Its contours capture
object boundaries well, but many of them, such as wallpaper patterns and floor
seams, carry no depth discontinuity. This work uses these contours as candidates
for depth boundaries and learns to select those that carry a depth step. To our
knowledge, no prior work uses the contours of a segmentation model as candidates
for depth boundaries.

\subsection{Boundary metrics}

Pixel-averaged metrics such as AbsRel and $\delta_1$ hardly reflect blurred
boundaries, since boundary pixels are a small fraction of the image. Depth
Pro~\cite{depthpro} proposed a scale-invariant boundary metric, \bfone{}, that
declares a depth step where the ratio of neighbouring depths exceeds a threshold,
sweeps that threshold from 5\% to 25\%, and scores the agreement with F1.
SMD-Nets~\cite{smdnets} proposed the Soft Edge Error (SEE) for evaluating stereo,
measuring the error at ground-truth boundary pixels while tolerating small
displacements, and Marigold V2~\cite{marigoldv2} also uses it. We use both and
propose no new metric.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centerline{\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/pipeline.png}}
\caption{Pipeline of the method. From the input RGB image, the frozen depth
model predicts depth and the frozen segmentation model extracts candidate
contours. The selector maps the image, the depth and the contours to a
probability for each contour pixel and keeps only a fixed fraction of the most
probable. Sharpening is applied at the kept contours alone, leaving every other
pixel unchanged. Only the selector is trained.}
\label{fig:pipeline}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Overview}

Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline} gives an overview of the method. The input is a single
RGB image. The depth model predicts affine-invariant depth, and the segmentation
model extracts candidate contours from region borders. The selector takes the
image, the predicted depth and the contours, outputs a probability for each
contour pixel, and keeps a fixed fraction of the most probable. Finally, the
barrier sharpening of Sec.~\ref{sec:repair} is applied at the kept contours. Only
the selector is trained: the depth model and the segmentation model are frozen,
and barrier sharpening has no learned parameters. Contours are selected because
many of them, such as wallpaper patterns and floor seams, carry no depth step,
and sharpening there creates a discontinuity the scene does not have
(Sec.~\ref{sec:why}).

\subsection{Barrier sharpening}
\label{sec:repair}

Diffusion-based depth estimators mix foreground and background depth at object
boundaries, turning a step that should change within one pixel into a slope over
several. If the boundary is known, the step can be restored by discarding the
mixed pixels around it and refilling each side from its own depth alone. We take
the boundary from the contours of a segmentation model and, because each contour
acts as a barrier to interpolation, call this operation \emph{barrier sharpening}.

Let $z$ be a predicted depth map, $V$ its valid pixels and $C \subseteq V$ a set
of contour pixels. The operation discards the band
\begin{equation}
B = \{\, p \in V \mid \mathrm{dist}(p, C) \le r_b \,\},
\end{equation}
labels the connected components of $V \setminus B$, so that the two sides of a
contour receive different labels, and floods those labels inward one pixel at a
time until $B$ is covered, giving every $p \in B$ a label $\ell(p)$. Each
discarded pixel is then refilled from its own side only,
\begin{equation}
\hat z(p) = \frac{1}{|N(p)|} \sum_{q \in N(p)} z(q),
\end{equation}
where $N(p) = \{\, q \notin B \mid \|q - p\| \le r_f,\ \ell(q) = \ell(p) \,\}$.
Pixels outside $B$ are unchanged. The component labels come from the contours
themselves, not from the segmentation masks. The operation has only two
settings, the band width $r_b$ and the refill radius $r_f$, and learns nothing.
We require $r_f > r_b$, or $N(p)$ is empty and the operation leaves the map
unchanged.

The separation of the two sides holds only where a contour closes or reaches the
image border. Where it ends inside the image the valid region stays connected
around that end, so both sides of the band belong to one component there and
one-sided interpolation does not hold. Selection makes this more common, since
keeping only part of the contour pixels leaves fragments rather than whole
outlines. Both effects weaken the repair, and both are present in every number
we report.

\subsection{The selector}
\label{sec:selector}

\textbf{Inputs and output.} The selector is a U-Net that takes the RGB image
(3 channels), the predicted depth (1) and the contour mask (1), and emits one
logit per pixel. The prediction is standardised per image. For a model that
emits disparity $v$ rather than log depth we apply $-\log\max(v, 10^{-3})$
first: disparity rises as depth falls, so feeding it raw would invert the
signal, and an affine-invariant disparity can be non-positive, which the clamp
handles. This is monotone in depth but not an affine map of log depth: the
unknown shift in $v$ survives the logarithm as a nonlinearity that per-image
standardisation cannot remove. We apply the selector unchanged regardless, so
the Lotus results in Table~\ref{tab:main} include the effect of this
approximation.

\textbf{Retention.} Rather than thresholding the probability, we keep a fixed
fraction $\rho$ of the contour pixels per image, in order of probability. A
fixed threshold keeps a different amount depending on how many contours a scene
happens to contain, so the budget the repair spends would drift from frame to
frame. $\rho$ is set to the share of positive contour pixels in the training
data.

\textbf{Labels and loss.} A contour pixel is positive if it lies within 1\,px of
a depth discontinuity in ground truth, where a discontinuity is a step whose
ratio of neighbouring depths exceeds a threshold $\tau$. The selector is trained
with binary cross-entropy averaged over the contour pixels only, since pixels off
the contours are never candidates. Ground truth is used only to generate these
labels and never appears in the inference path.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Setup}

We evaluate on the 654-frame NYUv2~\cite{nyuv2} test split, scene-disjoint from
the training frames. The two transfer sets of Sec.~\ref{sec:transfer} are the
800-frame ScanNet~\cite{scannet} validation sample used by~\cite{marigoldv2} and
a 300-frame scene-level hold-out of Hypersim~\cite{hypersim}. All three store
depth as 16-bit millimetres and are read with each dataset's own validity mask
and depth range; NYUv2 additionally uses the Eigen crop. The metric is the
scale-invariant boundary \bfone{} of Depth Pro~\cite{depthpro}. We report the
relative change in \bfone{} against each base model's own unsharpened
prediction. Predictions are aligned to ground truth in their own space before
scoring (log depth for Marigold V2, disparity for Lotus) and the alignment
affects every variant equally.

\textbf{Depth models.} We evaluate two models that are as far apart as the family
allows: Marigold V2, a 21.3B-parameter diffusion transformer fine-tuned with
4-bit QLoRA that emits affine-invariant log depth, and Lotus, an 868M-parameter
UNet that emits disparity. Neither reports \bfone{} on NYUv2, so we measure the
baselines ourselves.

\textbf{Choice of benchmarks.} \bfone{} compares neighbouring
pixels, so it needs ground truth dense enough for both to be valid, and three of
the five benchmarks these models are evaluated on do not provide it: KITTI
\cite{kitti} leaves 13.2\% of adjacent pairs valid and ETH3D \cite{eth3d} 4.1\%,
while DIODE \cite{diode} is dense but its evaluation protocol masks the depth
gradients this method operates on, removing 99.4\% of indoor discontinuities. We
therefore evaluate on NYUv2 and ScanNet, and additionally on Hypersim as a third
transfer set.

\textbf{Settings of the method.} Barrier sharpening uses $r_b = 2$ and
$r_f = 3$. The selector is a four-level U-Net with 1.9M parameters. On the
training frames 6.066\% of contour pixels lie within 1\,px of a true step, so the
retention is $\rho = 6\%$. The discontinuity threshold is $\tau = 10\%$, chosen on
a validation split rather than on the test set: scoring \bfone{} on the 141
frames of the 40 scenes held out of the 795 training frames, 10\% gives
$+12.74\%$ against $+8.66$, $+6.29$ and $+5.28\%$ for 15, 20 and 25\%. Raising it
thins the positives (from 6.07\% of contour pixels to 2.13\%) so the fixed budget
is then spent mostly on negatives. Every hyperparameter of the method was set on
the validation split; the one exception is a control, and
Sec.~\ref{sec:controls} states it there. The 795 NYUv2 training frames are split
by scene into 654 for training and 141 for validation; the deployed checkpoint is
the best-validation epoch and has seen only those 654, which are a different set
from the 654 test frames. Training takes 16 minutes and 3.5\,GB of VRAM on one
RTX 4090.

\textbf{Implementation.} The selector is trained with AdamW at a learning rate of
$3 \times 10^{-4}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$ and a cosine schedule, for 40 epochs at
batch size 4. Frames are used at their native $480 \times 640$ with no resizing and
no augmentation: the band and the refill radius are defined in pixels, so rescaling
would change what $r_b = 2$ means. Contours come from Segment Anything
\cite{sam} with the ViT-H checkpoint in automatic mode, a $48 \times 48$ prompt
grid and its default quality filter; ``grid 16'' and ``grid 32'' in
Sec.~\ref{sec:cost} vary that grid. A frame's contour set is the union of the
one-pixel boundaries of the returned masks, not the union of the masks, which
would fill the frame. Timings are on one RTX 4090.

\subsection{Why selection is needed}
\label{sec:why}
\label{sec:gtsel}

Applied at every contour that Segment Anything draws on NYUv2, barrier
sharpening lowers Marigold V2's boundary \bfone{} by 1.90\%
(Table~\ref{tab:diagnosis}). This is specific to a model whose boundaries are
already reasonable: on Lotus, whose boundaries start blunter (baseline \bfone{}
0.0693 against 0.0884), the same unselected sharpening \emph{gains} 10.31\%
(Table~\ref{tab:controls}).

654 frames. Oracle selection uses ground truth to choose a subset of the
segmentation contours and moves none of them.}
\label{tab:diagnosis}
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
Contours sharpened & share kept & \bfone{} change \\
\midrule
all segmentation contours & 100\% & $-1.90\%$ \\

\bottomrule

Measuring from each true depth discontinuity to the nearest contour gives a
median of 1.00\,px, with 66.9\% within 1\,px and 76.9\% within 2\,px. The
contours are where the boundaries are, but 79.8\% of the contour pixels have no
depth step anywhere within an eight-pixel window; these are wallpaper patterns,
floor seams and poster edges. Sharpening there introduces a discontinuity into a
region that has none.

The 79.8\% uses a generous eight-pixel window; the selection below uses a strict
one-pixel tolerance. Of the 22{,}167 segmentation contour pixels in an average
frame (10.6\% of its valid pixels) 1{,}345, or 6.07\%, lie within 1\,px of a true
discontinuity. An oracle that keeps exactly those, and leaves every kept contour
where it is, moves the effect from $-1.90\%$ to $+43.39\%$
(Table~\ref{tab:diagnosis}). The loss is therefore attributable to which contours
are sharpened rather than to where they lie, and the selector of
Sec.~\ref{sec:selector} learns that selection.

\subsection{Evaluation with Soft Edge Error}

An improvement visible in one metric may be an improvement in that metric's
definition. We therefore also report Soft Edge Error \cite{smdnets}, the
boundary metric Marigold V2 adopts, transcribed from its implementation.
SEE$_k$ averages, over ground-truth boundary pixels, the smallest absolute error
between the prediction and any ground-truth depth in a $k\times k$
neighbourhood; it forgives a displaced boundary and punishes a blurred one.
Unlike \bfone{} it is an absolute error in depth units, so it is not
scale-invariant and the prediction must be aligned first. Lower is better.

The two metrics agree on every variant of both models: the selector improves

\subsection{Analysis}

This subsection analyses where the improvement comes from and how far it
holds. We first compare against training-free controls to confirm that the gain
comes from the learned selection. We then measure the contribution of the
segmentation model and test how the selector generalises to other datasets.
Finally, we report the cost.

\textbf{Controls.}\label{sec:controls} The gain could come from the sharpening rather than from the learning, or from
simply being sparse. Table~\ref{tab:controls} measures six training-free
alternatives, each on the base model's own predictions.

The edge-preserving filter row is the classical alternative: a guided or joint
bilateral filter with the image as guide. Both lose on both models, monotonically
in the radius, from $-4.75\%$ and $-7.15\%$ at the gentlest setting to
$-50.14\%$ and $-45.30\%$. A filter can only smooth,
and a smoothed boundary is what \bfone{} counts against.

The hand-crafted control thresholds a local descriptor of the predicted depth's
log-gradient. We select its feature by the final metric on the test split rather
than by ranking quality on held-out data: over four candidates the AUC ordering
and the \bfone{} ordering are \emph{inverted}, and the feature with the worst
AUC (0.801) gives the best \bfone{} ($+1.25\%$) while the best AUC (0.876) gives
the worst ($-2.59\%$). That gives the control an advantage the method does not
have, which makes the comparison conservative.

Both random rows spend exactly the budget the selector spends, taken from its
output frame by frame. Random-pixel selection is close on the two models
($-24.96\%$ and $-22.11\%$), which is what a control that measures the repair
operation rather than the depth model should do. Random blocks, which keeps the
contour contiguous, costs almost nothing on either and nothing at all on Lotus:
scattering the selection is most of what makes the pixel control so destructive,
and choosing badly on its own incurs almost no cost.

\textbf{Contribution of the segmentation model.} Removing the segmentation model entirely, so that the selector must find
boundaries itself from RGB and depth and every valid pixel is a candidate, gives
$+7.30\%$. Both halves of this ablation come from a single seed, so the figure
to compare against is that seed's $+12.49\%$: the segmentation is worth 5.19
points, 42\% of that run's gain. This contribution arises on the recall side rather than the precision side. On
segmentation contours the true steps number 1{,}345 pixels per frame and the
budget of 1{,}330 can cover 98.9\% of them; counting over the whole valid image
there are 2{,}941, and the same budget caps recall at 45.2\%. The segmentation
earns its contribution by \emph{shrinking the target set}.

On neither held-out dataset does the selector beat using every contour. What
fails is the transfer, not the datasets. A selector trained on 10{,}000 Hypersim
frames reaches $+5.05\%$ on Hypersim itself, against $+2.28\%$ for no selection,
at 88.1\% precision on a 22.8\% base rate; the same checkpoint reaches
$-2.23\%$ on NYUv2 and $+0.39\%$ on ScanNet. Hypersim is not saturated either (sharpening at its own GT contours gives $+39.49\%$) so the method works in
its domain as it does in NYUv2's, and stops working only when the domain changes
between training and use.

Three further remedies failed: dropping the RGB channels, sweeping the retention
from 2\% to 20\% on ScanNet, and training on an equal mixture of 795 NYUv2 and
795 Hypersim frames. Data volume does not account for the gap either. Cutting
NYUv2 to 393 frames costs 2.2 points of gain ($+11.92\%$ to $+9.72\%$, two
seeds), against the 9.1 points that separate in-domain from ScanNet.

\textbf{Precision does not order the rows.} The NYUv2-trained selector is more
precise on Hypersim than in its own domain (55.8\% against 50.2\%) yet scores
worst there, and precision is not comparable across datasets in any case: the
share of contour pixels on a real step is 6.07\% on NYUv2 and 22.8\% on
Hypersim.

\section{Conclusion}

This paper proposed a post-processing method that improves the object boundaries
blurred by diffusion-based monocular depth estimators without modifying the
depth model. It fixes barrier sharpening, which restores depth steps at the
contours of a segmentation model, and uses a small U-Net to select only the
contours it is applied to.

The experiments yield three findings. First, sharpening at every contour lowers
boundary accuracy, and the cause is not where the contours lie but that contours
carrying no depth step are sharpened too. Second, learning which contours to
sharpen improves the boundary \bfone{} of Marigold V2 on NYUv2 by 11.9\%, and the
same selector applied to Lotus without retraining improves it by 27.8\%;
training-free controls and classical filters do not achieve this, and Soft Edge
Error confirms it. Third, the selector transfers across depth models but not
across datasets.

\textbf{Limitations.} The oracle selection of Sec.~\ref{sec:gtsel} reaches
$+43.39\%$ against our $+11.92\%$; the gap lies in the precision of the
selection, 50.2\% against 100\%. For AbsRel and $\delta_1$ we claim only that
they do not regress. Supervision and \bfone{} both refer to ground-truth depth
steps; ground truth never enters inference, but the shared definition may bear on
how the result is read. Making the selection independent of the training domain
is the main open problem.
